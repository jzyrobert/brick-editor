1. I chose a guardian crouched over a warm orange egg, with tall copper-coloured wings, an emerald body, ivory horns and a tail curling around the foreground. The wings’ height and spread determined the silhouette; the raised head and large haunches determined the proportions. Curved slopes finished the muzzle, back, feet and tail, while repeated curves suggested body armour. “The requested subject within a meaningful setting” explicitly required context; the nest, flooded sanctuary and protective pose were my interpretation. “Use a shared baseplate or connected plate-built display base” explicitly required a shared foundation; the black border and basalt shore were aesthetic choices. “Support every assembly” influenced the body foundation, shoulder bridge, limb bases and plates beneath the head frills.

2. I deliberately used stepped construction for the wing outlines, but I did not intend a pixel-like overall style. Each wing consists of eight two-stud-wide panels with different upper and lower heights. The tail similarly uses adjoining curved caps over small core patches. I interpreted “Finish the subject’s visible skin intentionally” and “Use curves, slopes, inverted slopes and wedges when their real geometry matches” as reasons to sculpt the exterior. They did not require curves everywhere or hiding every stud: “Do not use a blanket zero-stud rule” explicitly says otherwise. Scenery was required, not something to remove. The guardian relationship, contrasting egg, asymmetric wing heights and recognisable horns, claws and throat plates should remain.

3. “Whole numbers only” and “only upright quarter-turns” restricted offsets and wing orientation. They prevented freely tilted assemblies, but did not require flat wings; choosing thin upright panels was my compromise. Search found both curved shaping parts and specialised dragon wings. I chose brick-built wings for scale and control, so catalogue coverage was not the main limitation. Listed footprints governed placement and turned stud-edge positions.

   Counting affected the surroundings and hidden structure. Checks returned 1,206, then 1,128, then 1,100 parts. I reduced paving and terrain, aligned several assemblies to brick courses, and added support beneath vulnerable surfaces. Code brevity encouraged reusable panel and patch functions, but no hard verbosity limit forced those forms.

   Zero overlap errors did not establish connectivity. Explicit parts remove massing beneath their occupied cells; two assemblies can consequently appear to meet without overlapping stud connections. The shoulder slab and wing roots needed a more convincing connection analysis than I performed. Tail patches at different heights also needed scrutiny beyond shared boundaries.

Visible detachment is plausible, but I cannot verify its appearance without images. I intended the shoulder mass and spanning plate layers to join the roots; that intention does not prove an adequate mechanical connection. Similar uncertainty applies to stacked body skins and adjoining tail patches.

   The guardian scene reading well is consistent with the composition, although I cannot independently assess its visual success. Without locations or definitions, I cannot identify their causes or equate them with the reviewer’s observations.

5. I recommend three small changes. First, return warning locations and affected parts alongside errors in existing checks. This would expose uncertainty, but noisy warnings could divert effort toward harmless cases. Second, clarify that “touching faces or adjoining occupied cells do not establish a stud connection.” That would sharpen support reasoning, but could encourage bulky reinforcement. Third, consolidate the repeated surface instructions and explicitly permit coherent stepped sculpture. That could reduce pressure to apply curves mechanically, but could also make poorly shaped voxel approximations easier to justify.
