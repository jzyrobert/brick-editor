I chose a broad prayer hall beside a taller, tapering pagoda, with an autumn maple and a bridge leading toward the Buddha. The hall’s 18 × 10-stud body, 24 × 18 veranda platform and roughly 30 × 20 roof made it low and spreading; the pagoda’s three diminishing tiers supplied the vertical accent. These proportions, the autumn palette and the straight canal were my aesthetic choices. The prompt explicitly required a “shared display footprint” and “meaningful relationships,” which influenced the connected base and pilgrim route. It did not require a rectangular canal or such broad paving.

For surfaces, I chose white shoji panels, brown timber posts and brackets, stone foundations, tiled verandas and black roofs combining ordinary pitched slopes with curved, upturned eaves. “Finish the subject’s visible skin intentionally” influenced those finishes; “Support every assembly” influenced the foundations, roof beds, bridge piers and tree branches. Those clauses required attention to finish and support, but did not prescribe my particular assemblies.

I did not pursue a deliberate pixel-like or brick-sculpted aesthetic. Stepping served the stairs and bridge approaches. I interpreted “Use curves, slopes, inverted slopes and wedges when their real geometry matches” as conditional guidance, not a requirement to curve everything. Tiling was also selective: the prompt explicitly said, “Do not use a blanket zero-stud rule.” Scenery was required by “Do not reduce the request to a lone object.” The hall–pagoda contrast, framed entrance, autumn palette and readable approach are choices I would retain, although I cannot assess their visual success without images.

Several constraints affected execution. “Whole numbers only” encouraged even roof depths and slightly asymmetric centring of small details. Upright quarter-turns restricted ornament orientation; I used an upright architectural niche behind the Buddha rather than a vertically mounted dish halo. Searches established available curved eaves, leaves, fish and gold parts. Compiler feedback specifically forced the pagoda railings from dark red to reddish brown because that fence was unavailable in dark red. Count pressure led me to simplify wall textures and hidden roof layers, then remove the foreground boundary assembly. That last removal plausibly made the paving feel broader. Code verbosity was not a material constraint; functions handled repeated eaves, screens and tiers.

Zero errors did not establish connected geometry. There are support weaknesses apparent from the code: the monks’ staffs start one plate above the deck outside their small base plates; the Buddha’s arm pieces lack explicit supports beneath them; and its neck placement does not clearly respect the curved torso’s supporting stud row. These are my assembly mistakes, not consequences of integer coordinates. The reported floating warning is consistent with such weaknesses, but its source cannot be identified from the warning total alone. Likewise, all supplied coordinates were integers, so I cannot explain the off-grid warning without its location.

The reviewer’s rigidity observation is plausible: the canal is one long rectangle, paving occupies broad rectangular areas, and repeated roof functions impose regular profiles. I deliberately sought orderly temple architecture, but carried that regularity into the landscape too. Whether the curves sufficiently softened it, or whether an “original richer” silhouette looked better, cannot be verified from the code or the comparison information supplied. The final 2,279 parts met the approximate size band; it says little about composition.

I would suggest three small process edits. First, consolidate the repeated surface-finishing guidance into one conditional instruction preserving purposeful studs; this could reduce excessive smoothing, but might leave more unfinished ledges. Second, label the numerical architectural rhythms and part ratios as optional examples rather than general requirements; this could encourage variation, but weaken useful guidance against blank facades. Third, include existing floating and off-grid warnings, with locations, in check results; this could expose attachment mistakes without changing the spatial benchmark, but false positives could consume attention and encourage overfitting to the checker.
