I chose a working treeport: a timber-podded space shuttle beside an elevated control hut, with a log bridge leading to a radar nest. The 64 × 48 base, tall tree and low, broad shuttle determined the composition. The tree’s 10-stud trunk rose 81 plates before its upper branches; the shuttle occupied a 32 × 32 plot. That strong height contrast was my aesthetic choice. “Compose the subject and its environment as one cohesive diorama” explicitly required their shared setting; the fueling connection and radio operator were my interpretation of a meaningful activity.

The blend depended on blue and grey spacecraft surfaces, yellow glazing, printed space insignia, brown log siding and foliage. “Theme first” influenced my searches for those signature parts. “Build an economical connected core and a deliberately shaped exterior” influenced the packed cores, curved nose, wedge wing edges and curved nacelle covers. The tree received repeated steep slopes as bark ribs, while five reusable foliage crowns established its upper silhouette. Those particular assemblies were my choices, including their excessive regularity.

I did not intend a pixel-like treatment. I accepted stepped construction for branches and terrain, but aimed for shaped manufactured surfaces. The prompt encouraged curves and tiles where appropriate; it did not require hiding every stud, and explicitly said “Do not use a blanket zero-stud rule.” I retained studs on foliage and bridge boards. It required a shared base, but the black display border was my addition. Nothing required removing scenery. The integrated setting, contrasting heights, yellow glazing and printed insignia should remain.

“Whole numbers only” and “only upright quarter-turns” restricted offsets and orientation. I consequently used horizontal leaf assemblies and an upward-facing radar dish, although those constraints did not require broad, flat crowns. Searches supplied leaf footprints and body reach, but did not establish their usable stud locations. They also returned a sculpted Ewok head without a listed printed variant, limiting facial colour detail. Verified part colours guided the blue logos, brown shaping parts and green foliage. Count feedback kept the build near the target without adding filler. Code compactness encouraged reuse, but it did not force identical bark courses.

Compiler feedback materially changed the supports. I removed bark pieces near windows and roof surfaces, shortened fence posts and raised the buggy chassis above its wheels. I also replaced explicit upper branch columns with massing cylinders: parts could then cut their occupied cells out of that massing. This removed overlap errors, but did not establish that the remaining branch material connected every foliage assembly. Likewise, footprint overlap beneath a leaf does not necessarily mean its actual attachment studs meet. The accepted check reported 2,955 parts and no errors; it supplied no warnings.

The reviewer’s clear landing scene and forest/space blend are consistent with those choices, although I cannot independently confirm their visual success. Broad, flat canopy layers are plausible: the crowns use horizontal leaves stacked at several heights, with similar footprints. Repetitive trunk detail is directly supported by the code’s regularly spaced ribs and repeated six-plate courses. The newly supplied clash, floating and off-grid warnings show that acceptance was narrower than full physical validation. Without their locations, I cannot diagnose them. Generated wheel rims appeared at fractional coordinates in an earlier check, making wheel hardware a plausible source of the off-grid warning, not a confirmed one.

I would recommend three small process edits:

1. Include existing warning records and their locations in the text-only check response. This would expose actionable uncertainty, but noisy warnings could encourage unnecessary simplification.
2. Clarify that massing cutouts and zero overlap errors do not certify attachment. This would discourage treating compiler acceptance as structural proof, but could make builders overly conservative about legitimate overhangs.
3. Consolidate the repeated surface-finishing guidance into one instruction favouring coherent, subject-appropriate surfaces, including intentional stepped or studded treatments. This could reduce formulaic slope-and-tile decoration, but removing emphasis could also leave more unfinished surfaces.
