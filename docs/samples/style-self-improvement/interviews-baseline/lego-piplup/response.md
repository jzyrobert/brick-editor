I chose a standalone, waving character with an oversized navy head, projecting white cheeks, a yellow beak, two chest buttons and broad yellow feet. The raised flipper was my aesthetic choice to give it personality. “Silhouette first” and “Structure → shaped shell → focal details” materially influenced the construction: hollow cores inside repeated bands of curved and inverted slopes. “Vehicles can stand on wheels, creatures on feet” supported using the feet directly, without a display base. Omitting scenery was my judgment about this request, rather than a requirement to remove context.

I did not intentionally choose a pixel-like or stepped sculpture. I intended the successive curved bands to form a rounded shell, but their discrete transitions remained part of the construction. I read “Use curves, slopes … when their real geometry matches” as encouragement to search for shaping parts, not a requirement to curve every surface. Likewise, “Do not use a blanket zero-stud rule” explicitly allowed purposeful studs. The palette, facial features, waving gesture and separate feet are choices I would retain; their visual success cannot be established without images.

The strongest compromises came from counting and proportions. I initially estimated the structure too cheaply: the first check returned 1,428 parts. Subsequent reductions removed body bands, shortened the head’s middle course and narrowed some assemblies. The head’s maximum width became 26 studs, while its vertical span became approximately 27 plates. Those are different physical units, and I did not adequately account for that difference when judging roundness. The compression was my design error, aggravated by budget cuts—not something the prompt required. Medium azure lacked the searched 75-degree slopes, which influenced the body’s use of shorter 65-degree slopes. Integer coordinates and quarter-turns limited subtle centering and posing, but neither forced these proportions. Code verbosity was not a meaningful constraint.

The final internal change replaced several solid slabs with two-layer plate diaphragms and small central support patches. I expected greater savings than the reported final count of 1,204 demonstrates. That final revision was submitted after the three checks were exhausted. Even the preceding error-free check established neither complete attachment nor structural strength: slopes offer restricted attachment rows, and a plate spanning a hollow core needs connected support. My intended load path through feet, body, neck and head was not independently verified.

The reviewer’s wide, compressed-head observation is strongly plausible from those dimensions. Curved cladding cannot correct an unsuitable overall envelope. Inconsistent seams are also plausible: the code mixes curved bands, tiled corner patches, explicit bricks and plate cores, and some final savings removed previously tiled surfaces. I cannot verify their appearance, prominence or exact locations without images. I also cannot explain the off-grid warning from the supplied record.

I would recommend three small process edits:

- Clarify physical scale: “One stud horizontally is approximately 2.5 plates vertically.” This helps proportion planning, although it makes unit reasoning less demanding as a benchmark.
- Consolidate the repeated finish guidance into one statement allowing deliberate brick sculpting, curves and visible seams when coherent. This could reduce pressure toward curved cladding everywhere, but could also encourage underfinished surfaces.
- Include existing warning records in draft-check responses alongside errors and counts. This would expose attachment and clash concerns without another drafting stage, but could encourage overly conservative construction or distract from the design.
