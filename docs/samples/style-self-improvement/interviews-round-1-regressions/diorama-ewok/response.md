I chose a forest launch village: two tall trees carrying blue space huts, a connecting bridge, and a timber-bodied delta-wing ship below. The ship’s 32-stud wingspan, eight-stud-wide fuselage and paired yellow canopies supplied the classic-space silhouette. Brown framing, fern wing panels, acorn fuel stores and hooded figures supplied the Ewok interpretation. These followed “blend both sides in every element” and the theme guidance on “signature shapes, colours and parts.” The exact dimensions, dominant tree height and blue hip roofs were my aesthetic choices; the prompt did not require them.

“Compose the subject and its environment as one cohesive diorama” influenced the boarding ramp, fuel transfer and rover. “Use a shared baseplate or connected plate-built display base” explicitly required a shared foundation. Its black edging was my choice. Four landing supports carried the ship; rooted trunks and added crossbeams supported the decks. In hindsight, the allocation was uneven: the trunk-and-root section consumed 1,305 parts, while the canopy section received only 73. That was my design priority, not an unavoidable consequence of the brief.

I deliberately used stepped wing contours and brick-sculpted figures. The prompt explicitly allowed “stepped or pixel-like contours” and said to “Retain purposeful studs.” I did not interpret it as requiring curves, hiding every stud or removing scenery. Tiles on the manufactured wing surfaces followed the surface-finishing guidance; curved canopies and round ears were subject-specific choices. The shared setting, recognizable space palette, printed logos, differentiated heights and connected activity should remain.

Integer coordinates and quarter-turn-only placement encouraged upright fins, dishes and block-built characters rather than tilted assemblies. The searched catalogue supplied suitable yellow canopies and logo parts, so catalogue coverage was useful rather than a major limitation. Specific availability did affect execution: I replaced an unavailable transparent-yellow tile and changed unavailable black wheel rims to grey. Compiler feedback also drove wheel clearance, fence gaps and crew placement. Counting encouraged economical repeated assemblies, but 3,125 parts was comfortably within tolerance; it did not force sparse foliage. Code verbosity was not a binding constraint.

Zero errors established neither complete attachment nor sound construction. In particular, holes around trunks would leave surrounding decks unsupported without beams crossing beneath them; I added those beams in the final revision. Leaf attachment still depended on actual connection locations, and overlapping foliage footprints were not sufficient evidence of attachment. The subsequently reported eight clash warnings and one floating warning warrant investigation, but their locations were not supplied, so I cannot identify their causes.

The reviewer’s cylindrical-trunk and thin-foliage observations are plausible from the code. Both trees retained a constant eight-stud diameter, regular bark ribs and foliage concentrated into a few horizontal levels. Height and rib-count variation did little to change that underlying form. The rear would plausibly expose this repetition because it received much the same treatment as the front. The ship’s elevation and figures clearly express activity, but reduced character or finish is a visual judgment I cannot verify without images. I also cannot assess the independent baseline’s crown or overall superiority. It was not a reference I was preserving.

I would recommend three small process edits:

- Clarify that massing may form visible sculpture, but texture alone does not establish its contour. This could encourage stronger silhouettes, while potentially discouraging intentionally simple forms.
- Consolidate the repeated finishing guidance into one statement that studs, steps, tiles and curves are equally valid when coherent. This could reduce misplaced finishing effort, but weaken reminders to finish genuinely smooth surfaces.
- Include existing warning records and their source locations in draft-check responses, distinguishing warnings from errors. This would expose actionable uncertainty without changing the spatial task, but could encourage unnecessary changes in response to conservative warnings.
